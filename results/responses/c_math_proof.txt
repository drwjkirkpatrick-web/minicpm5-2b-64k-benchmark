Proof by Mathematical Induction.

**Base Case:**
For \( n = 1 \), the sum of the first 1 natural number is \( 1 \). The formula gives \( \frac{1(1+1)}{2} = \frac{2}{2} = 1 \). Thus, the base case holds.

**Inductive Hypothesis:**
Assume that for some arbitrary natural number \( k \), the formula holds, i.e., the sum of the first \( k \) natural numbers is \( \frac{k(k+1)}{2} \).

**Inductive Step:**
We must show that the formula also holds for \( k+1 \). The sum of the first \( k+1 \) natural numbers can be written as the sum of the first \( k \) natural numbers plus the \( (k+1)^{\text{th}} \) number. Using the inductive hypothesis:
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{k+1} i = \left( \sum_{i=1}^{k} i \right) + (k+1) = \frac{k(k+1)}{2} + (k+1).
\]
To simplify the right-hand side, we factor out \( (k+1) \):
\[
\frac{k(k+1)}{2} + (k+1) = (k+1) \left( \frac{k}{2} + 1 \right).
\]
Combining the terms inside the parentheses:
\[
\frac{k}{2} + 1 = \frac{k}{2} + \frac{2}{2} = \frac{k+2}{2}.
\]
Thus, the expression becomes:
\[
(k+1) \left( \frac{k+2}{2} \right) = \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}.
\]
This matches the formula \( \frac{(k+1)((k+1)+1)}{2} \). Therefore, the statement holds for \( k+1 \).

By the principle of mathematical induction, the formula \( \sum_{i=1}^{n} i = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} \) is true for all natural numbers \( n \).
